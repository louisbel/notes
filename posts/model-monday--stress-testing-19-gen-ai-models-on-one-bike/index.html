Gen AI for product creative: a field test

21 April 2026·1570 words·8 mins

▶ Watch the original video on LinkedIn

A honest, prompt-identical shoot-out between today’s image and video generation models, scored across a four-stage creative gauntlet built around a single product photo.

Why this test exists
#

Every week there’s a new “SOTA” model. Benchmarks on LMSys and Artificial Analysis tell you something, but they don’t tell you what actually matters when you’re a creative team trying to ship a campaign: does this model follow instructions, preserve brand marks, and stay physically plausible across a compound prompt?

So I built a test around a product brand I actually know intimately, the Cervélo S5 (the same bike Mathieu van der Poel rode to win Paris-Roubaix a few weeks ago), and ran 19 models through the same four-stage gauntlet.

One source image. Four stages of increasing complexity. Same prompts, same seeds where available, same aspect ratio. The question: when you turn the creative difficulty up, where does each model break first?

The gauntlet
#

Stage Prompt (paraphrased) Tests
1. Object Addition Add three accessories: bottle cage, saddle bag, radar unit. Don’t touch anything else. Instruction following, local edit isolation, brand-logo preservation
2. Background Transplant Move the bike onto wet Paris-Roubaix cobblestones, overcast, reflections in puddles. Full-scene re-lighting, material physics, subject preservation
3. Composite Mount the bike on the roof rack of an Audi RS6 Avant, parked on a forest gravel road. Cross-object scale, mounting geometry, reflections in car windows
4. Animation (video) Cinematic tracking shot of the car driving down the forest path, bike locked to the roof. Temporal coherence, physics over time, trademark-content policy

Stages one to three are image to image, stage four is image to video, using the best performing image output as the first frame for the video.

The roster
#

Image models (9): GPT Image, NanoBanana Pro, NanoBanana 2, Gemini 3 Pro, Seedream v5, Seedream v4.5, Flux Kontext, Flux 2 Pro, Reve.

Video models (10): Veo 3.1, Kling v2.1 Pro, WAN 2.1, MiniMax v01, Sora 2, Seedance v1 Lite, Seedance v2, Pixverse v4.5, Luma Ray 2, plus Veo 3 as baseline.

Findings, stage by stage
#

Stage 1. Object addition
#

The easiest stage on paper. Three small additions, leave everything else alone. It turns out “leave everything else alone” is where most models fail.

  • GPT Image nailed the bottle cage placement. Got the saddle bag right too. Radar unit was angled forward (it should face backward, that’s the whole point of a radar). Close to a pass.
  • NanoBanana Pro was the only model that correctly oriented the radar facing rearward. Missed the bottle cage entirely.
  • Gemini 3 Pro competent on all three, slightly plastic-y rendering on the saddlebag.
  • Flux Kontext placed accessories on wrong mount points: bottle cage on the seat tube instead of the down tube, saddle bag floating above the seat.
  • Seedream v4.5 went full bikepacking cosplay: added frame bags, handlebar rolls, straps everywhere. Classic over-interpretation, it sees “accessories” and maximises them instead of respecting the exact three-item list.

Nobody got all three accessories exactly right. Even in the simplest stage, instruction-count fidelity is not a solved problem.

Branding held up well here. Cervélo logos were preserved across most outputs.

Stage 2. Background transplant
#

This is where things got interesting.

  • Seedream v5, NanoBanana Pro, and Gemini all delivered genuinely beautiful wet-cobblestone scenes. Reflections in the puddles, gritty light, correct cold overcast palette. Indistinguishable from a location shoot on a bad-weather day.
  • GPT Image and Reve lost the Cervélo branding. Not partial, gone. Replaced with generic shapes or smooth logo-less tubing. If you’re a brand team, this is a hard fail.
  • Flux Kontext did something remarkable: it forgot the bike entirely. Rendered a gorgeous empty wet cobblestone road with nothing on it. The prompt got all the way to “move the bike” and the model ignored the subject and delivered the background.

The lesson: when the creative delta is large (indoor studio to outdoor wet scene), models compress information. Branding, logos, and small details are the first things that get compressed away.

Stage 3. Composite
#

This was the hardest image stage. The model has to simultaneously reason about:

  • Scale (bike vs car)
  • Mounting geometry (rack position, fork direction, strap points)
  • Reflections (car paint, forest canopy)
  • Surface physics (gravel, puddles, shadows)

Observations:

  • NanoBanana 2 and Seedream v5 nailed it. Correct proportions, realistic mounting angle, road surface detail, and, crucially, the reflection behavior on the Audi’s paint was plausible.
  • Flux Kontext rendered the bike about 1.5× too large and gave us an Audi from approximately 2008. Decent vibe, wrong era.
  • GPT Image laid the bike horizontally on the roof. Just… flat. Like a prop.
  • Flux 2 Pro refused the task outright. Returned what looked like a safety-filter decline: no roof rack, no bike on car.

This is the stage where prompt engineering matters most, and where model-native reasoning differences show up clearly. NanoBanana 2 and Seedream v5 are clearly using world-model reasoning about how a roof-mount works. GPT is pattern-matching “bike on car” and flattening to the likeliest image in training.

Stage 4. Animation
#

Ten video models. One cinematic prompt. This is where maintaining physics across frames separates the good models from the cheap ones.

Three questions for every output:

  1. Does the bike stay locked to the rack? (temporal rigidity)
  2. Do the spokes hold up as the wheels turn? (high-frequency detail coherence)
  3. Does the car actually move realistically on the trail surface? (ground-plane physics)

Observations:

  • Veo 3.1 won by a clear margin. Best in test. Smoothest motion, cleanest physics, most cinematic framing. It’s also the most expensive model by a significant factor. That checks out.
  • Kling v2.1 Pro a strong second. Smooth motion, bike stays put, reasonable trail feel.
  • WAN 2.1 had a weird flashing glitch mid-clip, looks like a failed temporal denoising pass. Hard to use.
  • MiniMax v01 started a full drag race. The prompt said “rolls down the forest path” and the model interpreted it as a dramatic acceleration-and-drift sequence. Entertaining, wrong.
  • Sora 2 rendered the scene beautifully, then refused to animate it. Almost certainly tied to the trademark-recognition guardrails OpenAI added after the launch-week Pokemon / Mickey Mouse incident. Every other model was fine animating a branded car with a branded bike; Sora alone would not.

And across all models, a recurring failure mode: bicycle spokes disintegrate under motion. Even in the best outputs. The combination of high-frequency thin structures, rotation, and compression is still a soft spot for every generative video model I tested. Watch closely; you’ll see it.

The verdict
#

Winner: Veo 3.1. Still wins after accounting for price, just barely.

Best value: NanoBanana Pro (image), Kling v2.1 Pro (video).

Most improved: Seedream v5. Genuine step-function from v4.5.

Most disappointing: Flux 2 Pro. “Refused” is not a result.

What I learned about Gen AI models, as of April 2026
#

A few patterns that only surfaced by running every model through the same four-stage escalation:

1. Instruction-count fidelity is still broken. No model reliably handles “do exactly N things and nothing else.” Most either under-count (miss one) or over-count (add extras). If your use case depends on a specific shopping-list of edits, plan for human QA.

2. Branding is the first thing to go. Under creative stress, logos get smoothed. If your product has distinctive type or marks (Cervélo’s cursive logo, a specific colorway), you will need either a mask-based workflow or a brand-locked LoRA. Hoping the model preserves it is not a plan.

3. “World-model” reasoning is a real differentiator. The gap between models that understand how a roof-mount works and models that pattern-match “bike on car” is enormous, and shows up only when you compose complex scenes. Simple single-edit benchmarks won’t catch it.

4. Trademark guardrails are now part of the product stack. Sora 2’s refusal is the first clear case where a policy decision is directly observable in video output. Expect more of this, and plan for it when you choose a model.

5. Thin high-frequency structures still break under video. Spokes, wires, mesh, thin text. If your product has any of these, test early.

6. Price does correlate with quality at the top end. Veo 3.1 is the most expensive and the best. The interesting question is how long that holds; Kling is closing fast.

What a team can actually do with this
#

If you’re a creative director, a brand manager, or a product marketing team evaluating Gen AI tooling, three practical ideas:

1. Run a gauntlet on your own product. Pick one hero asset, define three to four stages of increasing creative difficulty, and score every model on the same prompts. You’ll surface model-specific strengths and failure modes for your exact use case, not a generic benchmark.

2. Score with a rubric, not a vibe. Five axes, applied consistently: instruction adherence, subject preservation, brand preservation, physical plausibility, aesthetic quality.

3. Budget for the long tail of policy refusals. Sora 2’s trademark guardrail is the tip of the iceberg. Any production pipeline needs a fallback model selected before launch, not after a refusal surprises you in production.

What’s next
#

I’m planning to run this regularly. New models drop every few weeks; the ranking as of April 2026 will look quaint by Q3. The point of having a reproducible setup is that quarterly refreshes take about an hour of my time instead of a week.
