5 Things I Learned About AI and the Future of Work

29 June 2026·680 words·4 mins

“AI isn’t about AI, it’s about people.”

That was the opening line of a two-day gathering I had the chance to shadow at Carnegie Mellon, convened by the AI Adoption Initiative. The room was faculty, economists, government leads, and workforce experts from Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and Cisco. The framing came from Martial Hebert, CMU’s Dean of Computer Science, and it set the tone for everything after.

His other reminder: we were meeting almost 70 years after the Logic Theorist, the first program built to reason, was demonstrated a few buildings over. Seven decades in, the hard problem still isn’t the technology. It’s the people, the institutions, and the policy around it.

1. The critical role of facilitators
#

The U.S. didn’t pull ahead in the Second Industrial Revolution by inventing things. It pulled ahead by building a deep bench of facilitators — the people who took a new technology and put it to work solving real production problems.

“By training large numbers of facilitators and users who could effectively deploy and use such technologies to solve production problems, the U.S. was able to diffuse the benefits of mechanization more rapidly across its economy than countries that remained more focused on the innovation frontier.” — Ding, 2024

The parallel is hard to miss. The AI conversation is still stuck on replacing rather than augmenting, on the frontier rather than on diffusion. But the edge may not go to whoever builds the best model. It goes to whoever trains the most people to actually deploy it.

2. The layers of diffusion are permeable
#

Think of diffusion as three layers: innovators who build the models, facilitators who deploy, integrate, and validate them, and users who put AI to work day to day.

The catch, raised again and again, is that the layers are porous. The same person is often both a user and a facilitator — using AI to do their own job and helping colleagues deploy it. Some users are effectively innovators. That overlap makes policy and intervention more difficult.

3. You don’t have to own the technology to win from it
#

A country can shape real economic outcomes through national policy on adoption, even without a domestic frontier model. Several are betting exactly that.

  • Australia is doubling down on vocational training.
  • India is training the trainers — government programs that scale out through hundreds of local initiatives.
  • Singapore is building a fractional-CTO program to put AI expertise inside firms that can’t hire it full time.
  • Canada is scaling micro-credentials through non-profits to upskill educators.

One aside stuck with me: an automation tax is counterproductive. It penalizes incumbents heavy in human capital over newer firms that never carried that headcount — quietly punishing the employers doing the most to retrain people.

4. Education gets good news and bad news
#

The good news is almost a relief. The durable skills are the deeply human ones: critical thinking, collaboration, communication, ethical reasoning. The stuff AI doesn’t hand you. Call it the revenge of the Arts degree.

The bad news is structural. Higher ed isn’t known to be nimble, and what we need to teach is changing faster than institutions tend to move. Planning the skills of the future is, frankly, a difficult forecasting problem. The honest answer wasn’t a perfect curriculum — it was rapid feedback loops: surfacing what works quickly.

5. Retaining Critical Thinking & Persistence
#

The line I keep coming back to came from the education track:

“AI has become the easy button for students. Educators need to teach the value of persistence and the value in the struggle that comes with critical thinking.”

The point isn’t to ban the shortcut. It’s that the value of an education was always in the friction. The struggle is where critical thinking gets built, and where a student finds their own voice.

The open question is which kinds of friction are worth keeping anyway. Because some of it isn’t inefficiency. It’s how critical thinking is learned.
